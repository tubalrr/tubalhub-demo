01 / BREATHING

Box Breathing 4–4–4–4

A four-part breathing pattern commonly associated with U.S. Navy SEAL training. The four equal counts help give your attention one simple rhythm to follow.

🌬️ US Navy association

One minute of steady counting

Inhale through your nose for 4 seconds. Hold for 4 seconds. Exhale through your mouth for 4 seconds. Hold after the exhale for 4 seconds. Repeat for 4 rounds.

Slow breathing can change heart-rate variability and support parasympathetic activity; it is a relaxation tool, not a treatment for a medical condition.

Source: Cleveland Clinic ↗

02 / SLOW BREATHING

Tiyan Hinga Diaphragmatic breathing

Place one hand on your chest and one on your belly. On an easy inhale, let the belly rise more than the chest. Aim for about 5–6 slow breaths per minute without forcing the pace.

03 / RELAXATION

4–7–8 Breathing Dr. Andrew Weil

A paced breathing exercise taught by Dr. Andrew Weil: inhale quietly through the nose for 4, hold for 7, then exhale through the mouth with a gentle “whoosh” for 8.

04 / FIVE SENSES

5–4–3–2–1 Grounding

A five-senses grounding exercise can bring attention away from racing thoughts and back to what is happening around you right now.

The point is not to get every number “right.” The exercise is about deliberately noticing your senses and anchoring attention in the present.

06 / SLEEP HYGIENE

Sleep Tips WHO-informed basics

Sleep habits work better as a routine than a one-night hack. WHO guidance includes consistent sleep and wake times, a dark and comfortable sleep environment, and avoiding caffeine before bedtime.

⏰

Keep a consistent wake time

Get up at around the same time each morning, including weekends, as much as practical.

🌙

Dim light before bed

Make the last hour calmer: reduce bright light and screen use so your environment signals that the day is winding down.

☕

Watch caffeine timing

WHO advises avoiding caffeine before bedtime. A 2 pm cutoff can be a simple personal rule for an earlier bedtime, but it is not a WHO temperature-or-time requirement.

🛏️

Cool, dark, quiet room

A comfortable temperature, darkness, and less noise can support a better sleep setting. Around 18–22 °C is a common practical target, not a specific WHO rule.

📚 Source: World Health Organization — stress and sleep-hygiene guidance. Read WHO guidance ↗
